The three numbers

No one can tell you exactly what a player will score, so the board gives you a range instead of a single guess. Proj is the most likely score: as many weeks will come in above it as below it. Floor is a bad week and Ceiling a great one; about 8 weeks in 10 land somewhere between them. The Mean is the average of all possible outcomes. It runs a little higher than Proj because big games pull the average up. Use Proj to compare two players for a start/sit decision, and the Mean when you are adding up a whole lineup or comparing with ESPN or Yahoo.

Where the number comes from

Four things, in order of how much they matter:

  • How much he's been used. Carries, targets, and his share of the team's work over the last three games, with extra weight on red-zone and goal-line chances. Whether he is the lead back or lead receiver, and whether a teammate who normally takes work is out this week.
  • Who he is. Where he was drafted, how many years he's been in the league, and how productive he was per touch last season. This is how the model tells the difference between a backup who's filling in and a talented young player who has taken the job.
  • The game. The point spread and expected total from the betting line, how fast the team plays, and how often it throws.
  • The opponent and the weather. Every defense is measured by what it has done to the quarterbacks it has faced, compared with how those quarterbacks played against everyone else. A defense that has made good quarterbacks look ordinary costs points for the quarterback and every receiver and back catching passes against it, equally. That's the Opp adj column. Wind takes a little more off passing. A defense that gives up long runs raises a running back's ceiling but not his most likely score.

The model deliberately does not use a lot of things you'll see elsewhere, because we tested them and they didn't predict anything: "points allowed to wide receivers," matchups against particular corners, coaching tendencies, offensive-line rankings. Each week's projections are checked against what actually happened and against the dumbest possible alternative, a player's season average; the running record is kept in the project's findings file.

Tiers and the Usage label

Tier groups players by this week's rank on Proj: RB1 means a top-12 projection, RB2 means 13 to 24, and so on. The letter breaks each group where there's a real gap in the numbers: RB1-a players are clearly ahead of RB1-b players, while two players in the same letter are close enough to call a coin flip.

Usage is a separate label that ignores the projection and only looks at how much the player has been on the field and getting the ball lately: elite, high, mid, or low. The useful cases are where the two disagree. A player with elite usage but a modest projection is someone getting a lot of work and not doing much with it; the model doubts the output but the opportunity is real. A player with a strong projection and low usage is someone the model believes in for reasons beyond recent work, usually pedigree or a role that just changed. Those are the rows to think hardest about.

Flags

  • emerging: the player has been producing well above his season average over the last three games, and he wasn't already a top-12 player on the year. Something has changed recently: a bigger role, a teammate out, a new quarterback, or a hot streak. Open the row to see which. History says most of these settle about halfway back; the ones that stick are usually high draft picks or players producing efficiently with the extra work, not just getting it.
  • soft run D: the opponent gives up long runs at a high rate. The back's ceiling is higher this week; his most likely score isn't.
  • shootout: two good offenses, a close line, and no top defense. Games like this go over the total far more often than usual.
  • tough pass D: the opponent is among the hardest defenses in the league to throw against, by what it has done to real quarterbacks this season.
  • wind: 12 mph or more forecast at kickoff. extreme weather: heavy rain or very high wind; the passing game may barely happen, and that cuts the quarterback and receivers hard.
  • new QB: a different quarterback is starting this week. Receivers lose about 2.5 points on average in that first week, no matter who the new starter is.
  • teammate out: the usual lead back or lead receiver on his team is out, so there is more work to go around.
  • injury (hamstring, shoulder, illness, and so on): the body part listed on the injury report. The designation (Questionable, Limited) only tells you whether he plays; once he's active, the type of injury is what matters. Players who play through a hamstring, a shoulder, a wrist, or an illness have averaged 1.5 to 3 points below their projection; an ankle or a knee for a receiver costs nothing. The adjustment shown next to the injury is applied to the projection, sized from those records and scaled down where the record is thin.

All data is free and public. The board is rebuilt every evening after the injury reports come out, and again Saturday with the final designations and weather.
